How this paycheck calculator works

Your gross salary is never what lands in your bank account. This calculator estimates your real take-home pay by subtracting the four things that come out of nearly every American paycheck: federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and — in most states — state income tax. If you contribute to a traditional 401(k), that comes out before income tax too, which lowers your taxable income.

Federal income tax is calculated on a progressive basis using the 2026 brackets, after subtracting the standard deduction and any pre-tax 401(k) contribution. Social Security is withheld at 6.2% up to the annual wage base, and Medicare at 1.45% on all wages, with an extra 0.9% on high earnings. State income tax is then applied using your state's rate structure.

Key terms explained

Gross vs. net pay. Gross is your salary before any deductions. Net — your take-home pay — is what remains after taxes and pre-tax benefits.

Federal income tax. Charged in brackets: only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate, so a raise never costs you money overall. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly.

FICA (Social Security + Medicare). A flat payroll tax. Social Security applies only up to the wage base ($184,500 in 2026); earnings above that are not subject to the 6.2%. Medicare has no cap, and high earners pay an additional 0.9% above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married).

State income tax. Varies widely. Nine states levy none at all; others use a flat rate or progressive brackets. Our state figures are simplified estimates and exclude local city taxes and state-specific credits.

Where you live can change your take-home pay by thousands of dollars a year on the same salary. A six-figure earner in Texas or Florida keeps noticeably more than the same earner in California or New York, simply because of state income tax. Worked example: $100,000 in five different states

To see how much geography matters, take a single filer earning $100,000 with no 401(k) contribution. Federal tax and FICA are identical everywhere: about $13,170 in federal income tax (2026 brackets, after the $16,100 standard deduction), $6,200 in Social Security, and $1,450 in Medicare. Common paycheck surprises

The mid-year Social Security bump. If you earn above the $184,500 wage base, the 6.2% Social Security deduction stops partway through the year — and your take-home pay suddenly rises. It isn't a raise; the same amount resumes every January.

Bonuses withheld at a flat rate. Employers typically withhold 22% federal on bonuses regardless of your bracket. If your marginal rate is higher, you'll owe the difference at filing — the same trap that hits RSU income, and worth planning for in a bonus-heavy year.

Two jobs, one standard deduction. Each employer withholds as if theirs is your only income, so dual-income households and job-switchers often discover both employers applied the low brackets. The IRS W-4 has a checkbox for exactly this — use it if your household has more than one paycheck.

Frequently asked questions

How is take-home pay calculated?
Take-home pay is your gross salary minus federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare (FICA), state income tax, and any pre-tax deductions like a 401(k). What remains is your net pay.
Which states have no income tax?
Nine states have no state income tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. In these states your take-home pay is higher because only federal tax and FICA are withheld.
What is FICA?
FICA is the payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare. Social Security is 6.2% on wages up to an annual cap ($184,500 in 2026); Medicare is 1.45% on all wages, plus an extra 0.9% on high earnings.
Does a 401(k) lower my taxes?
Yes. Traditional 401(k) contributions are deducted from your pay before federal and state income tax, lowering your taxable income. They do not, however, reduce Social Security or Medicare tax.
Why is my take-home pay lower than my salary?
Your gross salary is reduced by federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax, and any pre-tax benefits. Combined, these can take 20% to 40% of gross pay depending on your income and state.
